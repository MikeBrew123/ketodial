Recalculate Your Keto Macros Every 10 Pounds

Marcus · Performance Coach · July 31, 2026

Your scale stopped moving. You didn't cheat. You're still under 20 grams of carbs. You're still tracking every gram of fat.

And nothing's happening.

Here's what most people miss. You're not failing the diet. You're running numbers from a body that doesn't exist anymore.

Most stalls are stale math. You set your macros at 250 pounds. You're 218 now. Those numbers were built for a guy who's gone.

What Actually Changes When You Lose 30 Pounds

Let's run it. Male, 250 pounds, 5'10", 40 years old, lightly active. Mifflin-St Jeor puts his BMR around 2,050 calories. Multiply by 1.375 for activity and you get a maintenance of roughly 2,820. Take a 20 percent cut and your target lands near 2,250.

Now drop him to 220 pounds. Same height, same age, same activity. BMR falls to about 1,915. Maintenance drops to 2,630. Your 20 percent target is now 2,100.

That's a 150 calorie gap. Every single day. On paper.

But the paper is optimistic. Leibel and Rosenbaum published data in the New England Journal of Medicine back in 1995 showing that after a 10 percent weight loss, total energy expenditure runs 10 to 15 percent below what the equations predict. Your body gets more efficient. Non-exercise movement drops without you noticing. You fidget less. You take the elevator.

Stack that on top of the 150. Now you're eating 300 to 400 calories above your actual target and calling it a plateau.

Why Keto Makes This Easy To Fix

This is the part I like about keto specifically. You've only got one variable to move.

  • Carbs stay fixed. Twenty grams of net carbs at 250 pounds. Twenty grams at 190. Ketosis doesn't care what you weigh.
  • Protein stays high. Set it at 0.8 to 1.0 grams per pound of your goal weight, not your current weight. For our guy aiming at 190, that's 150 to 190 grams. It shouldn't drop as you shrink. If anything, protecting lean mass in a deficit matters more the leaner you get.
  • Fat is the dial. It's the remainder. It absorbs every adjustment.

So the recalculation is simple. New target of 2,100 calories, minus 175 grams of protein (700 calories), minus 20 grams of carbs (80 calories), leaves 1,320 calories for fat. That's 147 grams, down from 163.

Sixteen grams of fat. About a tablespoon and a half of butter.

That's it. That's the whole stall. A tablespoon and a half of butter.

The 10-Pound Trigger Protocol

Stop waiting for a stall to tell you something's wrong. By the time the scale flatlines you've already been overfed for weeks. Use a trigger instead.

Here's the protocol I give every client:

  • Step 1. Write down your calculation weight. That's the number you plugged into the calculator on day one. Put it somewhere you'll see it.
  • Step 2. Weigh yourself daily, same conditions. Morning, after the bathroom, before food. Take the seven day average. Daily readings on keto are noise, water and sodium swings will lie to you constantly.
  • Step 3. When your seven day average sits 10 pounds below your calculation weight, you recalculate. Not when you feel like it. Not when you get frustrated. At 10 pounds.
  • Step 4. Run the KetoDial calculator again with your new weight. Same activity level, same goal setting. Keep protein anchored to goal weight. Let fat take the hit.
  • Step 5. Hold the new numbers 14 days minimum before you judge them. Glycogen and water will muddy the first week. Always do.
  • Step 6. Update your calculation weight. Repeat at the next 10.

Ten pounds is roughly 50 calories of pure equation drift plus another 50 or so of adaptation. Small enough that the adjustment never feels punishing. Frequent enough that you never drift 400 calories off target.

Every Third Recalculation, Take a Break

At 30 pounds down, don't cut. Eat at maintenance for seven to ten days.

The MATADOR trial out of Australia in 2018 tested this directly. One group dieted straight through for 16 weeks. The other alternated two weeks of deficit with two weeks at maintenance. The break group lost more fat and held onto more of their metabolic rate. Same total deficit, better outcome.

You're not losing progress during a break. You're protecting the machine that produces the progress.

What Not To Do

Don't cut fat at random. This is the default move and it's a mess. Someone stalls, panics, and slashes 500 calories of fat overnight. Now they're eating 1,600 calories, they're cold, they're sleeping badly, and their training goes in the toilet.

Don't let protein fall. When people eyeball a cut, protein is usually what gets sacrificed because it's the expensive part of the plate. Wrong direction. Protein is the last thing you touch.

Don't recalculate weekly. I've had clients try it. Two of ten found the 10-pound trigger too frequent and it fed the obsession, so we moved them to 15 pounds. Fine. The point is a fixed rule, not a specific number.

And don't forget your electrolytes scale with your intake, not just your body. When calories come down, so does incidental sodium. That's where a lot of "recalculation fatigue" actually comes from. It's not the deficit. It's 1,500 milligrams of missing sodium.

Run It Now

Pull up your current seven day average. Compare it to the weight you originally entered.

If the gap is 10 pounds or more, you already know what to do. Recalculate, adjust the fat, hold it two weeks.

Protocol beats guessing. Every time.

Your mileage may vary.
