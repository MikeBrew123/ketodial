Keto Flu Is Not Inevitable: The Electrolyte Fix

Sarah · Health Coach · May 30, 2026

You Don't Have to Feel Terrible

Every week I get some version of the same message: "I started keto and I feel awful. Headaches, muscle cramps, brain fog, zero energy. Is this normal?"

It's common. But it's not normal, and it's definitely not inevitable.

What most people call "keto flu" isn't your body rejecting ketosis. It's not a detox reaction. It's not carb withdrawal. It's an electrolyte deficiency, and it has a straightforward fix. The fact that so many people suffer through it unnecessarily is one of the reasons I wanted to write this.

What's Actually Happening in Your Body

When you cut carbohydrates, your insulin levels drop. That's the whole point. Lower insulin means your body starts burning fat for fuel instead of glucose. Great.

But insulin also tells your kidneys to hold on to sodium. When insulin drops, your kidneys start flushing sodium out at a much faster rate. This is why you lose several pounds of water weight in the first week of keto. That water is following the sodium out.

And here's the cascade that causes the problem: when sodium drops, your body also loses potassium and magnesium to compensate. Within 48-72 hours of starting keto, you can be significantly depleted in all three electrolytes. That depletion is what causes every symptom people associate with "keto flu."

  • Low sodium: headaches, fatigue, dizziness, brain fog, nausea
  • Low potassium: muscle cramps, weakness, heart palpitations, constipation
  • Low magnesium: muscle twitches, insomnia, anxiety, cramps (especially at night)

This isn't speculation. The relationship between carbohydrate restriction, insulin, and renal sodium excretion is well-documented in the medical literature. Your body isn't broken. It's just missing minerals it used to get from a different dietary pattern.

Why "Just Eat More Salt" Isn't the Whole Story

The most common advice you'll see online is "salt everything." It isn't wrong. For a lot of people it's most of the answer. But it's worth understanding why it works, because that's what tells you whether it's the whole answer for you.

Here's the part people miss. When you were eating a standard diet, most of your sodium wasn't coming from the salt shaker. It was coming from bread, sauces, deli meat, canned soup, crackers, and takeout. Cut all of that in a single week and you've quietly removed the largest source of sodium in your day without ever deciding to. That's the change your body is reacting to, and it's why food that used to taste fine now tastes like nothing.

The other part: sodium is one of three minerals on the move here. Potassium and magnesium are shifting too, and neither of those comes out of a salt shaker. Salt on its own is like fixing one flat tire on a car with three. You'll feel somewhat better and still lie awake wondering why your calves are cramping at two in the morning.

Before You Add Anything, Read This Part

This comes before the helpful section on purpose, because the order matters more than people expect.

Potassium is the one that catches people out. If you take a daily pill for your blood pressure or your heart, a water pill, or anything to do with your kidneys, check with your doctor or your pharmacist before you add potassium from a supplement, from a salt substitute like Lite Salt or No Salt, or from a daily electrolyte packet. Some of those medicines already hold potassium in your body, which means extra on top is the problem rather than the help. Have the same conversation if a blood test has ever come back with a note about your potassium, your sodium, or your kidney function, or if anyone has ever told you to watch your salt.

If you're reading that and thinking you're honestly not sure whether any of it is you, that's a reason to ask, not a reason to assume you're fine. It's a smaller errand than it sounds. Take the actual bottles, or the printout from the pharmacy, and ask: "I've started a low-carb diet and I'm losing salt. Is there anything on this list I should be careful with?" A pharmacist can answer that in about two minutes and you don't need an appointment.

Separate from potassium, and worth its own sentence: keto often lowers blood pressure on its own. If you take medication for high blood pressure, your doctor should know you've started, because the dose you're on was set for the way you used to eat.

Salting your food to taste is a smaller and different question than adding a supplement or a salt substitute, and for most people it isn't the one at issue. But if you have been told to limit sodium, that instruction still stands, and nothing on this page overrides it.

What Actually Helps

I've been coaching people through keto adaptation for years. Here's what makes the difference, roughly in the order it makes it. You'll notice it's mostly food and mostly boring. That's the honest version.

Salt your food, and then salt it a bit more. On the eggs, on the steak, into the vegetables, in the broth, at every meal, until it tastes right to you. Your appetite for salt tends to climb for the first few weeks after you cut carbs and then settle down on its own. If your food has started tasting flat, that's information, not a craving to resist. Taste is a better instrument than people give it credit for, and it's the one you already own.

Put potassium on the plate rather than in a glass. Avocado, spinach, salmon, mushrooms, and plain unsweetened yogurt if you eat dairy are all solid keto-friendly sources. This is the one mineral where eating the food genuinely beats anything you can stir into water, and it's also the one where the supplement route is the route with the medication question attached. Food first isn't a slogan here. It happens to be both the safer road and the more effective one.

Eat full meals, not half ones. This is the step people skip in week one and it's the one that does the most work. Appetite often falls off a cliff in the first few days, so you eat half a dinner because you're not that hungry, and then day three feels like a wall. Minerals ride in on food. Skip the food and you skip the minerals, whatever is in your water bottle.

Magnesium is the honest exception. It's the hardest of the three to get enough of from food, which is why it's the one people ask me about most. It's also the one where the right amount depends on you, on your kidney function, and on everything else you already take, so it's a good question for a pharmacist who can look at your actual list. What I will say is that form matters more than most people realize. Magnesium oxide is cheap and poorly absorbed. Glycinate and citrate absorb better, and citrate is the one most likely to loosen your stools. Take that much to the pharmacy counter and let someone who can see your medications tell you the rest. I'm not going to put a number on a page that strangers read.

Drink to thirst, not to a target. Pouring plain water on top of falling sodium is how people accidentally make this week worse, and "drink a gallon a day" advice is the most common route to it. Water alongside salted food, not instead of it.

What About Ketoade and Electrolyte Powders?

This page used to carry a ketoade recipe, measured out by the quarter teaspoon. I've taken it down, and I'd rather tell you why than quietly swap it for something else.

Two reasons. The first is that a measured mineral drink is exactly the wrong thing to hand to everyone who lands on a page, because the readers it suits worst are the least likely to know that it doesn't suit them. A salt substitute is a potassium supplement wearing a kitchen label. The second reason is simpler: it was never the part that worked. Salted food and full meals were doing the heavy lifting, and the drink was collecting the credit.

If you like something savoury to sip, salted broth is a genuinely pleasant thing to have in the afternoon and it's food. If you're eyeing a commercial electrolyte powder, turn it over and read the potassium line, and if anything in the section above sounded like you, that label is the thing you bring to your pharmacist. "Is this one all right for me?" is a question they can answer in about a minute.

How to Read What Your Body Is Telling You

Symptoms are useful signals. They're just not dials to crank. Here's how I read the common ones.

Lightheaded when you stand up, foggy, headachey, still wiped out after a full night's sleep. That's the classic week-one sodium picture, and it usually answers to salted food and a proper meal within a day or two. If it hasn't shifted after a few days of eating real meals and salting them to taste, that's a call to your doctor rather than something to escalate on your own at home.

Cramping calves and feet, especially at night, eye twitches, trouble falling asleep. Usually potassium and magnesium, and usually better once real food is back on the plate consistently. If it's stubborn, magnesium is the pharmacist conversation and anything beyond that is a doctor conversation.

Heart palpitations, a racing or skipping heartbeat, or muscle weakness that feels new. I've pulled this one out on its own because it doesn't belong with the others. Both too little and too much potassium can cause it, and you cannot tell which from the way it feels. So this is not one to answer by adding potassium. Call your doctor and tell them you've recently changed how you eat. If it arrives with chest pain, breathlessness, fainting, or a heartbeat that won't settle, that's urgent care today, not a nutrition question.

Loose stools. Usually magnesium citrate, or a lot of salt landing at once. Spread things across the day, and mention it to whoever recommended the supplement.

Why Most People Quit in Week One

Here's what makes me frustrated about the state of keto advice online. People start keto, feel terrible for five days because nobody told them about electrolytes, and conclude that keto isn't for them. They quit and go back to eating the way that was making them sick in the first place.

They didn't fail at keto. They failed at hydration and mineral balance. That's it. The diet itself was working. Insulin was dropping, fat burning was starting, the metabolic shift was happening. But the electrolyte depletion made them feel so bad that they couldn't push through.

If that happened to you before, try again. This time, salt your food properly and eat full meals from day one instead of waiting until you feel bad. Most people notice a real difference by day three.

And if you haven't started keto yet and you're worried about the "keto flu," now you know. It's not a rite of passage. It's a mineral deficit with a simple solution. Get your macros right, get your electrolytes right, and the first week doesn't have to be miserable.
